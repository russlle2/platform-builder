Common myths about therapy — and what research actually shows

Myth: Therapy is only for major crises

Truth: Short-term therapy often helps with common distress—sleep disruption, work stress, relational patterns—before they become crises. Interventions are focused and goal-oriented when appropriate.

Myth: Talking is all it takes

Truth: Talk is coupled with practical strategies, skill-building and measurable goals. I integrate evidence-based techniques tailored to your needs.

Myth: You’ll be judged — Truth: Confidential, nonjudgmental stance.
Myth: Therapy is too long — Truth: Many clients see meaningful change in months.
Myth: One method fits all — Truth: I blend approaches to fit your context.

Core pillars of my practice

Evidence-informed practice

I rely on empirically supported methods (e.g., CBT, trauma-focused CBT, relational models) while tailoring them to your situation.

Collaborative planning

We set clear objectives together and measure progress. You remain the expert in your life; I bring structure and tools.

Context-sensitive care

Interventions consider culture, identity, neurodiversity and practical constraints like work or caregiving.

Illustrative examples (anonymized)

Reframing work stress

Client: Mid-career professional with chronic anxiety about performance. Approach: Skills-focused plan (sleep hygiene, boundary-setting, brief exposure tasks). Outcome: Reduced panic episodes and clearer work–life limits within 3 months.

Recovering from relational trauma

Client: Person navigating trust after relationship betrayal. Approach: Trauma-aware pacing, relational skill-building, somatic regulation. Outcome: Improved safety in relationships and emotional regulation over longer-term work.

Transition and purpose

Client: Recently relocated, struggling with meaning and routine. Approach: Values clarification, activity scheduling and community resources. Outcome: Increased engagement and gradual social connection.
