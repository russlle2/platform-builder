Common assumptions I hear — and a different way to think about them

Myth: Therapy is only for 'severe' problems

Truth: Therapy can be useful for everyday struggles, decision points, and preventing problems from escalating. It's a space to practice new ways of relating to yourself and others.

Myth: I should 'feel better' fast

Truth: Change often happens gradually. We focus on learning practical skills and building a reliable approach you can use between sessions.

Myth: All therapists are the same

Truth: Clinical orientation, lived experience, and interpersonal fit matter. I use trauma-informed, attachment-aware, and CBT-informed interventions tailored to you.

Myth: Sharing everything is required

Truth: You control pacing and boundaries. We negotiate what feels safe and relevant while keeping confidentiality and professional limits clear.

Foundations of our work

Evidence-informed practice

Interventions are chosen based on research and adapted to your values and context — pragmatic, measurable steps toward clearer functioning.

Collaborative stance

We set goals together, review progress, and revise plans. Your feedback shapes the work; therapy is a partnership, not a prescription.

Practical skill-building

We practice strategies you can use between sessions: grounding, cognitive reframing, communication exercises, and pacing for sustainable change.

Illustrative vignettes (anonymized)

Relearning boundaries after burnout

A mid-career professional came to therapy exhausted and disconnected. We mapped patterns that drained energy, tried micro-boundary experiments, and practiced self-directed scheduling. Within months they reported improved energy and clearer priorities.

Managing panic in daily life

A client with recurrent panic learned stepwise exposure and interoceptive exercises paired with cognitive restructuring. We focused on measurable goals (ride transit twice a week) and tracked reductions in avoidance over time.

Repairing a strained relationship

Using communication experiments and curiosity-based reflection, a couple improved shared decision-making and reduced reactive cycles. Work centered on concrete practices between sessions and a commitment to short check-ins.

Cases are anonymized and representative; outcomes vary. Therapy is not a guaranteed solution but a collaborative process.
